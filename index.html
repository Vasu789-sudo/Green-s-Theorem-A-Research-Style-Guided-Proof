Abstract

A one-page summary

Green’s theorem is the planar prototype of a general principle: the integral of a derivative over a region is determined by values on its boundary. This paper-style tour gives a complete proof for simple regions using only Fubini’s theorem and the fundamental theorem of calculus, extends it by decomposition to general and multiply connected regions, derives the flux form, and develops corollaries (area formulas, curl as circulation density, Green’s identities, Cauchy’s theorem). We then lift the same argument to Stokes’ theorem, the divergence theorem, and the generalized Stokes theorem. Every step is paired with a figure that animates on its own.

Context

Where the theorem comes from

The result is named for the English self-taught mathematician George Green, but the story is a relay. Green’s 1828 essay on electricity and magnetism contained the three-dimensional integral identities that bear his name; the planar statement used today is commonly credited to Cauchy and Riemann. Dates and attributions below are as usually cited.

1828

George Green

Privately publishes An Essay on the Application of Mathematical Analysis to the Theories of Electricity and Magnetism, introducing the potential and the integral identities now named after him.

1846

Cauchy

Writes an integral over a closed curve as an integral over the enclosed area, the planar form, in his work on complex integration.

1851

Riemann

Uses and proves the planar theorem in his doctoral dissertation on functions of a complex variable.

1854

Stokes

Poses the curl theorem as a Cambridge exam question, after learning of it from Kelvin; it is now called Stokes’ theorem.

1900s

Poincaré · Cartan

Differential forms and the exterior derivative unify all of these results into a single statement.

Why does it matter? It converts a problem about the whole region into one about its edge, or vice versa. That trade is the engine behind conservation laws, potential theory, and Cauchy’s theorem, and it is the reason a surveyor can find an area by walking its perimeter.

Preliminaries

Fields, work, curl and divergence

A planar vector field is a pair F = (P, Q) of functions on a region. Its work along a curve C is the line integral of its tangential component, independent of how C is parametrized (up to direction):

∫C F·dr = ∫C P dx + Q dythe integrand of Green’s theorem

Two local quantities describe how a field behaves near a point. Watch both: a paddle wheel spins at a rate set by the curl, and a ring of dust swells at a rate set by the divergence.

curlz F = ⟦F|∂Q|∂x⟧ − ⟦F|∂P|∂y⟧    div F = ⟦F|∂P|∂x⟧ + ⟦F|∂Q|∂y⟧spin per unit area  ·  expansion per unit area

The geometry also needs care. A Jordan curve is a simple closed curve; it splits the plane into an inside and an outside. A curve is positively oriented when the inside lies to the left as you travel. We assume the boundary is piecewise C¹, so the line integrals above make sense.

Reading guide. Parts 1–3 below are the heart of the proof. Parts 4–6 stretch it to harder regions and to the flux form. The later sections show where the result leads.
The theorem

The statement

Let D ⊂ ℝ² be a region whose boundary C = ∂D is a positively oriented (counter-clockwise), piecewise-smooth, simple closed curve. If P, Q are C¹ on an open set containing D, then:

∮C P dx + Q dy
=
∬D ( ⟦F|∂Q|∂x⟧ − ⟦F|∂P|∂y⟧ ) dAcirculation around the boundary = total curl inside
🌀

Circulation form

The loop integral of F = (P,Q) equals the integral of scalar curl ∂ₓQ − ∂ᵧP over D.

💧

Flux form

∮ F·n ds = ∬ div F dA — proved in Part 6.

📐

Forms

With ω = P dx + Q dy: ∮∂D ω = ∬D dω.

Big picture

Why it’s true: cancellation

Chop D into tiny cells. Around each cell the circulation is ≈ curl × area. Add them up: each interior edge is traversed twice in opposite directions and cancels, leaving only the outer boundary.

Σ curl · ΔA → ∬D curl dAthe rest of this page makes that cancellation rigorous
Complete proof

The proof, in seven parts

We prove the theorem for simple regions by two lemmas, then extend it to general regions, regions with holes, and the flux form.

Part 0

Groundwork: definitions and tools

Move the dot around the curve. “Positive orientation” means the interior is always on your left.

1

For a smooth parametrization t ↦ (x(t),y(t)), t ∈ [α,β], the line integral is defined by

∫C Pdx + Qdy = ⟦I|β|α⟧ [ P x′(t) + Q y′(t) ] dtreversing direction flips the sign; piecewise curves add
2

Type I: D = {a ≤ x ≤ b, g₁(x) ≤ y ≤ g₂(x)}. Type II: D = {c ≤ y ≤ d, h₁(y) ≤ x ≤ h₂(y)}. A region that is both is simple (disks, ellipses, triangles, convex sets).

3

Two tools: Fubini’s theorem, and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus on each vertical (or horizontal) slice:

⟦I|g₂(x)|g₁(x)⟧ ⟦F|∂P|∂y⟧ dy = P(x, g₂(x)) − P(x, g₁(x))
Part 1

Lemma A — ∮ P dx = −∬ ∂yP dA on a type I region

Sweep a vertical strip across D. Each strip’s integral collapses to the difference of P at its top and bottom — exactly the values the boundary curves C₃ and C₁ “see”.

1

Since ∂P/∂y is continuous on compact D, Fubini applies:

∬D ⟦F|∂P|∂y⟧ dA = ⟦I|b|a⟧ ⟦I|g₂(x)|g₁(x)⟧ ⟦F|∂P|∂y⟧ dy dx
2

Apply the FTC to the inner integral:

= ⟦I|b|a⟧ [ P(x,g₂(x)) − P(x,g₁(x)) ] dx
3

Now compute ∮C P dx piece by piece. On C₁ (bottom, left to right): x = t, y = g₁(t). On C₃ (top, traversed right to left) we get a minus sign. On the vertical sides C₂, C₄, x is constant so dx = 0:

∫C₁ P dx = ⟦I|b|a⟧ P(t,g₁(t)) dt
∫C₃ P dx = − ⟦I|b|a⟧ P(t,g₂(t)) dt
∫C₂ = ∫C₄ = 0
4

Add the four pieces and compare with Step 2:

∮C P dx = − ∬D ⟦F|∂P|∂y⟧ dA
∎ Lemma A
Part 2

Lemma B — ∮ Q dy = +∬ ∂xQ dA on a type II region

Same idea, rotated: sweep a horizontal strip. Now the left and right curves matter, and the sign flips because counter-clockwise means the right side goes up.

1

Fubini, integrating in x first, then the FTC:

∬D ⟦F|∂Q|∂x⟧ dA = ⟦I|d|c⟧ [ Q(h₂(y),y) − Q(h₁(y),y) ] dy
2

Boundary: the right curve x = h₂(y) is traversed upward (+), the left curve x = h₁(y) downward (−), and on the horizontal sides dy = 0:

∮C Q dy = ⟦I|d|c⟧ Q(h₂(y),y) dy − ⟦I|d|c⟧ Q(h₁(y),y) dy
3

The right-hand sides match:

∮C Q dy = + ∬D ⟦F|∂Q|∂x⟧ dA
∎ Lemma B
Part 3

Combine: Green’s theorem on simple regions

If D is both type I and type II, both lemmas hold on the same curve C. Add them.

∮ P dx + ∮ Q dy = − ∬ ⟦F|∂P|∂y⟧ dA + ∬ ⟦F|∂Q|∂x⟧ dA
⟹ ∮ P dx + Q dy = ∬ ( ⟦F|∂Q|∂x⟧ − ⟦F|∂P|∂y⟧ ) dA  ∎

Check it numerically. Pick a field and an ellipse. The tool computes both sides by brute force and shows the curl as a heat map (coral positive, blue negative).


Part 4

General regions: cut, apply, glue

Many useful regions aren’t simple. Split D into finitely many simple pieces D₁,…,Dm that overlap only along shared arcs. Drag the slider to pull the pieces apart, then cancel the shared edges.

1

Green’s theorem holds on each piece (Part 3). Sum over k:

Σk ∮∂Dk P dx + Q dy = Σk ∬Dk ( Qx − Py ) dA = ∬D ( Qx − Py ) dA
2

A shared arc Γ appears in two pieces, with opposite orientations, so its two contributions are negatives: ∫Γ + ∫−Γ = 0. Only arcs on ∂D remain, each once, counter-clockwise.

Σk ∮∂Dk = ∮∂D interior arcs cancel in pairs
∎ General regions
Part 5

Regions with holes

For an annulus, cut a bridge so the region becomes simply connected. Shrink the cut: the two sides of the bridge are traversed in opposite directions and cancel.

1

The cut region is simply connected, so Part 4 applies, with boundary = outer arc + bridge + inner arc (clockwise) + bridge.

2

Let the gap → 0. The bridge terms cancel, the arcs close up, and the inner circle keeps its clockwise orientation:

∮outer, CCW + ∮inner, CW = ∬D ( Qx − Py ) dA
∮outer − ∮inner, CCW = ∬D curl
∎ Multiply connected regions
Part 6

The flux (normal) form

Watch the tangent T and outward normal n travel around the curve. The normal is the tangent rotated 90° clockwise.

1

With T = (x′, y′)/|r′| and ds = |r′| dt, the outward unit normal is n = (y′, −x′)/|r′|, so

n ds = ( dy, −dx )
2

For F = (M,N):

∮ F·n ds = ∮ M dy − N dx
3

Apply Green with P = −N and Q = M:

∮ F·n ds = ∬D ( ⟦F|∂M|∂x⟧ + ⟦F|∂N|∂y⟧ ) dA = ∬D div F dA
∎ Flux form
Corollaries

Consequences and extensions

Once the theorem is in hand, a surprising amount follows in a line or two.

Corollary 1

Area as a boundary integral

Choose (P,Q) so that Qx − Py = 1. Three natural choices give three formulas, all equal to the area of D:

∮ x dy = − ∮ y dx = ½ ∮ x dy − y dx = Area(D)(P,Q) = (0,x), (−y,0), (−y/2, x/2)
→

For a polygon with vertices (xi, yi) each edge is a line segment, and the third formula collapses to the shoelace formula:

Area = ½ Σi ( xi yi+1 − xi+1 yi )
Corollary 2

Curl is circulation density

Let Cr be the circle of radius r about a point p. Green’s theorem and the mean value theorem for integrals (the curl is continuous) give:

curlz F(p) = limr→0 ⟦F|1|πr²⟧ ∮Cr F·drthis is why the paddle wheel in the preliminaries measures curl

The same argument on the flux form gives div F(p) = lim (1/πr²) ∮ F·n ds, a coordinate-free definition of divergence.

Corollary 3

Green’s identities

Apply the flux form to F = f∇g, where div(f∇g) = ∇f·∇g + f∇²g:

∬D ( f∇²g + ∇f·∇g ) dA = ∮C f ⟦F|∂g|∂n⟧ dsGreen’s first identity

Swap f and g and subtract; the symmetric gradient terms cancel:

∬D ( f∇²g − g∇²f ) dA = ∮C ( f ⟦F|∂g|∂n⟧ − g ⟦F|∂f|∂n⟧ ) dsGreen’s second identity

Consequence: a harmonic function (∇²u = 0) is determined inside by its boundary data, and ∮ ∂u/∂n ds = 0.

Corollary 4

Cauchy’s integral theorem

Write f = u + iv and dz = dx + i dy. Then ∮ f dz splits into two real line integrals, and Green turns each into an area integral:

∮ f dz = ∬ ( −vx − uy ) dA + i ∬ ( ux − vy ) dA

If f is holomorphic, the Cauchy–Riemann equations ux = vy and uy = −vx kill both integrands:

∮C f(z) dz = 0
Corollary 5

Path independence, potentials and winding numbers

If Qx = Py on a simply connected domain, Green gives ∮ = 0 for every simple loop, so line integrals depend only on endpoints and F = ∇f. If the domain has a hole, the test fails exactly as in the 2π example below. For closed curves that cross themselves, each point is counted by its winding number w:

∮C P dx + Q dy = ∬ w(x,y) ( Qx − Py ) dAa figure-eight has w = +1 on one lobe and −1 on the other
Corollary 6

How weak can the hypotheses be?

C¹ fields on a piecewise-C¹ region is the comfortable setting. The theorem survives much more: it holds on Lipschitz domains, for rectifiable Jordan curves, and, in the language of geometric measure theory, for sets of finite perimeter (the De Giorgi–Federer divergence theorem). What it cannot survive is a singularity inside the region, as the 2π example shows.

Worked examples

Ellipse area from a boundary integral

Take P = −y/2, Q = x/2, so Qx − Py = 1. Parametrize x = a cos t, y = b sin t.

Area = ½ ∮ x dy − y dx = ½ ⟦I|2π|0⟧ ab dt = πab

Triangle circulation

(0,0), (1,0), (1,1); P = xy, Q = x².

∫₀¹∫₀ˣ x dy dx = 1/3direct: 0 + 1 + (−2/3) = 1/3 ✓

Flux through a circle

F = (x,y), radius r.

div F = 2 ⟹ 2·πr² = 2πr²direct: F·n = r, length 2πr ✓

Astroid area

x = cos³t, y = sin³t.

½∮ x dy − y dx = (3/2) ⟦I|2π|0⟧ sin²t cos²t dt = 3π/8

Hard integral, easy curl

∮ (ex − y³) dx + (cos y + x³) dy on the unit circle.

curl = 3x² + 3y² ⟹ ⟦I|2π|0⟧ ⟦I|1|0⟧ 3r³ dr dθ = 3π/2
Care

The fine print

Holes break naive use. Take F = (−y, x)/(x²+y²). Its curl is 0 away from the origin, yet:
∮unit circle F·dr = 2π ≠ ∬ 0 dAF is not C¹ at the origin, so Green’s theorem does not apply to the disk

Conservative fields

If Qx = Py on a simply connected domain, every closed-loop integral vanishes, so F = ∇f.

Regularity

C¹ and piecewise-smooth C suffice; sharper versions use rectifiable curves and Jordan’s theorem.

Orientation slips

Clockwise traversal flips the sign. Inner boundaries of holes must run clockwise.

Singularities inside

If P or Q blow up inside D, the theorem does not apply to D; cut the singularity out first.

Self-crossing curves

Weight each region by its winding number (Corollary 5) rather than assuming +1.

Beyond Green · I

Stokes’ theorem: Green on a curved surface

Take Green’s region and bend it into space. A surface S with boundary curve ∂S carries a vector field F = (P,Q,R), and circulation around the edge again equals curl accumulated across the surface.

∮∂S F·dr
=
∬S ( ∇×F )·n dSorientation by the right-hand rule: thumb along n, fingers along ∂S

If S is a flat region in the xy-plane then n = k, (∇×F)·k = Qx − Py, and Stokes is Green. The hemisphere below rotates so you can see the boundary and the upward-pointing curl together.

Proof sketch
1

Parametrize S by r(u,v) over a planar region D; then ∂S is the image of ∂D and n dS = (ru × rv) du dv.

2

Pull the circulation back to the uv-plane, where it is an ordinary planar line integral:

∮∂S F·dr = ∮∂D (F·ru) du + (F·rv) dv
3

Apply Green’s theorem in D with P = F·ru and Q = F·rv. The second-derivative terms F·ruv appear in both and cancel:

= ∬D [ ( ∂uF )·rv − ( ∂vF )·ru ] du dv
4

With A the Jacobian of F, the integrand is rvᵀ(A − Aᵀ)ru. The antisymmetric part of A encodes the curl:

( A − Aᵀ ) w = ( ∇×F ) × w ⟹ integrand = ( ∇×F )·( ru × rv )
∎ Stokes

Curl of a gradient

If F = ∇f then the circulation around any closed loop is 0, so ∇×∇f = 0.

Surface independence

Two surfaces with the same boundary give the same flux of the curl; over a closed surface it is 0.

Faraday’s law

∮ E·dr = −dΦB/dt is Stokes applied to ∇×E = −∂B/∂t.

Beyond Green · II

The divergence theorem: Green’s flux form in 3D

Lift the flux form one dimension. For a solid V with outward-oriented boundary surface ∂V, the total outflow equals the total expansion inside. It was found repeatedly: Lagrange (1762), Gauss (1813), Ostrogradsky (1826), Green (1828).

∯∂V F·n dS
=
∭V ∇·F dVnet flux out through the surface = total source strength inside
Proof sketch
1

Write F = (P,Q,R). It suffices to prove ∯ R k·n dS = ∭ ∂zR dV and the two analogues, then add.

2

Let V = {(x,y) ∈ D, u₁ ≤ z ≤ u₂}. Fubini and the FTC, exactly as in Lemma A:

∭V ⟦F|∂R|∂z⟧ dV = ∬D [ R(x,y,u₂) − R(x,y,u₁) ] dA
3

On the top surface k·n dS = +dA, on the bottom it is −dA, and on the vertical sides k·n = 0. So the surface integral equals the same expression.

∯ R k·n dS = ∬D [ R(x,y,u₂) − R(x,y,u₁) ] dA
4

Add the three components; decompose general solids into simple ones, and interior faces cancel in pairs, just as in Part 4.

∎ Divergence theorem

Gauss’s law

∯ E·n dS = Q/ε₀ is the divergence theorem applied to ∇·E = ρ/ε₀.

Continuity equation

Mass conservation, ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρv) = 0, follows by letting the volume shrink to a point.

3D Green identities

Same derivation as Corollary 3 with F = f∇g on a solid.

The unification

The generalized Stokes theorem

All the results above are one statement. For an oriented n-dimensional region Ω with boundary ∂Ω and a smooth (n−1)-form ω:

∫∂Ω ω = ∫Ω dωintegrate the derivative over the inside, or the form over the boundary

The animation shows the lowest rung, the fundamental theorem of calculus: the area accumulating under f′ equals the change of f across the endpoints, which is “the integral of ω = f over the two-point boundary.”

Dimension 1

⟦I|b|a⟧ f′ dx = f(b) − f(a)

The FTC.

Plane

∮ P dx + Q dy = ∬ ( Qx − Py ) dA

Green: ω = P dx + Q dy, dω = ( Qx − Py ) dx∧dy.

Surface in space

∮ F·dr = ∬ ( ∇×F )·n dS

Stokes: a 1-form on a 2-surface.

Solid in space

∯ F·n dS = ∭ ∇·F dV

Gauss: a 2-form on a 3-solid.

Why it works
1

On the unit cube, take ω = f dx₂∧…∧dxn. Then dω = ∂₁f dx₁∧…∧dxn, and by Fubini and the FTC in x₁:

∫cube dω = ∫ [ f(1,…) − f(0,…) ] dx₂…dxn
2

These are the two opposite faces, with opposite induced orientations; all other faces contribute 0 because dx₂∧…∧dxn vanishes on them. That is Lemma A in n dimensions.

3

Charts, pullback (which commutes with d) and a partition of unity carry the cube case to any manifold; shared faces cancel as in Parts 4 and 5.

4

Applying the theorem twice gives the deepest consequence: d² = 0, which is the same fact as ∂∂ = ∅, the boundary of a boundary is empty:

d(dω) = 0 ⟺ ∇×(∇f) = 0   and   ∇·(∇×F) = 0
Conclusion

What we have shown

Green’s theorem says that the circulation of a field around a closed curve equals the total curl enclosed. We proved it from two ingredients: Fubini’s theorem to slice the region, and the fundamental theorem of calculus to evaluate each slice. Two lemmas (one per coordinate direction) combine on simple regions; cutting extends the result to general regions and regions with holes; a 90° rotation of the field gives the flux form.

1 · The mechanism

Slice, integrate, and the endpoints of each slice are exactly the boundary values.

2 · Orientation matters

Counter-clockwise boundary, outward normal, right-hand rule: one sign convention throughout.

3 · Cancellation scales up

Interior edges cancel in pairs, which handles arbitrary regions and holes.

4 · One theorem, many names

Green, Stokes, Gauss, and the FTC are the same statement in different dimensions.

∮∂D ω = ∬D dωthe whole page in one line
